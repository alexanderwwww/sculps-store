# Authenticity and Risk

Instructions for the machine. `SKILL.md` already states the rule: everything listed is
genuine and provable, replicas are never listed at any price, and the app never
authenticates. This file is the operational detail behind that rule — the specific
tells to ask Alex about, the documentation to photograph, how a claim actually runs,
and the shipping practice that survives one.

**The machine's role is fixed: it drafts questions and checklists for Alex, and it
writes what he confirms. It does not judge authenticity and it never writes a sentence
that asserts authenticity as a guarantee.**

Researched September 2026.

---

## 0. The three things that end the business

Flagged plainly, because they are not the same as a bad month.

1. **Listing a counterfeit.** Depop prohibits counterfeit goods outright. A verified
   report can suspend or permanently ban the account, and rights-holders (Nike,
   Hermès, Chrome Hearts) pursue sellers directly — Depop's own enforcement is
   reported as rising, with average takedown resolution of 3–7 business days.
   Counterfeit trafficking is a criminal matter in the EU, not only a platform
   matter. One fake is worth more downside than a year of margin.
2. **Repeat "significantly not as described" claims.** The buyer keeps the money and
   often the item; repeats kill the shop's standing and eventually the account.
3. **Moving a sale off-platform.** It voids every protection on both sides, is grounds
   for suspension, and is the standard opening of every scam run against resellers.

There is a fourth, slower one: **undeclared income.** See the DAC7 note in
`01-sourcing-and-margin.md`.

## 1. How a claim actually works — and the Greek complication

### If the buyer paid with Depop Payments
- **Depop Protection**: buyer must report within **30 days of the purchase date**.
- Grounds: item not received, or **significantly not as described (INAD)**.
- Covered amount: full purchase price **including shipping, fees and taxes/duties**.
- Route: Profile → Purchases → select order → Open a Dispute, with photos.
- Depop reviews, and if it finds for the buyer, the buyer returns the item and is
  refunded once the return is delivered. **Seller may be liable for return shipping.**
- The buyer must keep the item throughout; disposed items are not refunded.
- Refunds typically process in **2–5 business days** once approved.
- The Depop selling fee is returned to the seller when a refund is processed **in the
  app**.

### If the buyer paid via PayPal — which is Alex's rail from Greece
This is the part the blogs skip and it is the most important paragraph in this file.

Greek sellers are **paid through PayPal, not Depop Payments**. Sources indicate Depop
Protection is framed around Depop Payments, which means a material part of Alex's
exposure runs through **PayPal Buyer Protection** instead. **Unverified — Alex must
confirm in his own account which protection scheme applies to his orders before
relying on either.** Treat it as PayPal until proven otherwise.

Under PayPal:
- Buyer opens "Significantly Not As Described" within **30 days of delivery**, with an
  outer limit of **180 days from payment**.
- **PayPal Seller Protection does not cover "not as described" claims at all.** It
  covers unauthorised transactions and item-not-received. This is the single largest
  structural risk in the business: on an INAD claim from Greece, Alex's only defence is
  his own evidence.
- Card chargebacks can arrive **after** the PayPal window and are harder still.
- Signature confirmation: previously mandatory for Seller Protection on transactions
  **≥ $750**; as of **26 January 2026** PayPal recommends rather than requires it
  (source-reported; verify before relying on it). **Keep taking signature anyway on
  anything over €300** — it costs a few euro and it is the only proof of delivery that
  a card issuer respects.
- The item must be shipped to **the address on the transaction record**, no exceptions,
  ever, for any reason a buyer gives.

**Consequence for pricing:** because INAD is essentially undefended from Greece, the
listing itself is the defence. Everything in `02-listing-craft.md` about disclosing and
photographing flaws is not politeness — it is the entire risk control.

### What triggers claims, in order of frequency
1. **Undisclosed flaw** — a stain, hole, smell, or repair not in the photos.
2. **Measurement wrong or absent** — "doesn't fit" becomes "not as described" when the
   listing gave no tape.
3. **Colour off** — shot under warm indoor light, arrives grey.
4. **Smell** — smoke, damp, mothballs. Never survives.
5. **Alteration not disclosed** — taken-in waist, shortened sleeves, replaced zip.
6. **Authenticity doubt** on a designer piece.
7. **Slow or untracked shipping**, which converts an impatient buyer into a claimant.
8. **Buyer's remorse dressed as INAD** — the one you can fight, if the listing is good.

## 2. Evidence pack — shoot this before anything ships

For **every** item:
- All listing photos, originals, kept on disk with the SKU.
- One flat lay with a tape measure visible against the largest measurement.
- Every flaw, close, in daylight, numbered to match the description.
- **A photo of the packed parcel with the shipping label legible**, taken at the
  counter, before handover.
- The carrier receipt and the tracking number, saved with the order.

For **designer and high-ticket** (add):
- Every hallmark, stamp, serial, date code and hardware engraving, macro, unedited.
- The interior, the lining, the base, the corners.
- Any box, dust bag, clochette, keys, spare buttons, care card, receipt.
- **A short video** turning the item in daylight, unedited, timestamped by the file.
  Video is the strongest single piece of evidence in an INAD dispute because it is hard
  to fake and it shows condition continuously.
- A photo of the item next to that day's newspaper or a handwritten dated note if the
  value is over ~€1,000 (low cost, high credibility, unverified as a Depop-recognised
  practice but standard in luxury resale).

Keep all of it for **12 months** minimum — longer than the 180-day PayPal outer limit.

## 3. Brand-by-brand tells

These are **questions to ask Alex and things to photograph**, not a verdict engine.
The machine's output for a designer piece is a checklist, not a conclusion.

### Chrome Hearts
Among the most counterfeited brands on earth. One authentication service reports
**about 1 in 3** Chrome Hearts pieces submitted come back fake.

- **Hallmark placement**: rings on the interior band; pendants and earrings on the bail
  or the back face. Wrong location is a fail before you look at anything else.
- **Stamp quality**: genuine marks are **deeply die-struck with sharp, vertical walls**
  inside the characters. Fakes are shallow, rounded-edged (cast or laser-etched),
  blurred, or unevenly spaced.
- **`.925`**: on authentic pieces the dot sits noticeably **closer to the 9**.
- **Font**: Chrome Hearts gothic/blackletter, crisply struck. Counterfeiters copy the
  words, not the letterforms. Compare against a known-good photo of the same model,
  not against memory.
- **"CH Forever" spacing**: fakes crowd the letters; the "O" is thicker and clumsier
  than the authentic thinner form.
- **Crosses and daggers**: authentic motifs are **wider, thicker, bulkier**, sharply
  defined, symmetrical. Fakes are narrow, thin, uneven-edged.
- **Weight**: solid .925 sterling, heavy for its size. Light or hollow is a fail.
- **Magnet**: sterling silver is **not magnetic**. Any pull is a fail. (This is a
  negative test only — passing proves nothing.)
- **Patina**: authentic silver tarnishes unevenly and naturally. A uniform mirror
  shine, peeling, or plating wear is a fail.
- **Packaging**: branded pouch, box, sometimes certificate. Absence is not proof of
  fake; presence is not proof of real — counterfeiters copy packaging first.

Photograph for the listing: interior stamp macro, `.925` macro, motif front and
profile, weight on a scale in the frame, packaging.

### Nike (sneakers)
- **Style code (SKU)**: the 9-digit code on the **inner tongue/size tag** must match
  the **box label** must match the **shoe's printed size**. A mismatch across those
  three is the tell counterfeiters still get wrong most often.
- **Size tag**: check spacing, font weight, date format, country of production,
  barcode layout — the tag is the hardest single component to reproduce exactly.
- **Stitching**: authentic is tight, even, no loose threads, panels aligned,
  perforations regular. Fakes wander.
- **Insole**: correct length, not overlong, not over-glued; logo printed cleanly, not
  smudged or peeling.
- **2026 reality check**: counterfeit operations now run commercial-grade equipment.
  Visual inspection alone is no longer decisive on hyped models. **If the piece is a
  hyped model and provenance is thin, do not list it.**

### Nike (vintage apparel — dating, not authenticating)
Tag eras, earliest to latest: **pinwheel** (rarest, late 70s) → **orange tag** →
**blue tag** (mid-80s) → **grey with orange swoosh** (late 80s–1994) → **thin white**
(mid–late 90s) → **silver** (2000s, not vintage yet). The **swoosh did not appear on
neck tags until 1987**; a swoosh-less wordmark tag is a genuine early signal.
Orange tag + single stitch + "Made in USA" together is a strong early read.

### Carhartt
- **Date code**: many tags carry a 3–4 digit code where the last two digits are the
  **year** and the first one or two are the **month** — `598` = May 1998. Reliable from
  roughly the mid-1980s.
- **Union label + Made in USA** together places the piece **pre-1994**, before
  production moved offshore. The small red-and-blue circular union label carries a
  real resale premium — say so in the listing, it is a searched term.
- **Tag evolution**: 70s white cloth neck tags with the classic logo and **no model
  number** → white vinyl tags at the hem or neck carrying style codes (J97, J01) and
  fabric codes (BRN, BLK).
- **No chart maps a tag design to a single year.** Cross-reference tag style + union
  label + hardware + date code. "Made in USA" alone does not make it 1970s.
- Carhartt WIP (the European line) is a different product from Carhartt USA workwear —
  do not conflate them in a title; buyers search them separately.

### Hermès Birkin
This is the highest-risk object in the shop. **Default: do not list a Birkin without a
third-party authentication report from a recognised service, paid for by Alex, in
hand, photographed.** The cost is trivial against the downside.

- **Blind stamp**: colourless, heat-pressed into the leather on an interior strap or
  near the closure; indicates production year. Since **2016**, one row with a date
  letter plus a five-character alphanumeric string, with two letters below (the
  artisan ID).
- **Saddle stitching**: hand-done, even, with a characteristic **diagonal angle** and
  consistent tension. Stitch count varies by leather and size.
- **Heat stamp**: crisp `HERMÈS PARIS MADE IN FRANCE`.
- **Hardware**: engraved, `HERMÈS` on the lock, plated in precious metal, hallmark next
  to the "Paris" stamp on gold-plated pieces.
- **Completeness**: symmetrical clochette, lock, **two** keys.
- **Structure**: dense structured grain, smooth zip, protective base studs, correct
  weight.
- **The date code alone proves nothing.** Experts read font, embossing depth, stamp
  position and craftsmanship together. A correct-looking stamp on a bad bag is the
  standard modern fake.
- **Hermès does not authenticate for the public.** Any listing implying Hermès has
  verified it is false.

### Common vintage (dating, which is what buyers are really asking)
**T-shirts:**
- **Single stitch** on sleeve and bottom hem → 1970s to **early/mid 1990s**. Double
  stitch became standard mid-to-late 90s.
- Era blanks: Screen Stars, Hanes, Fruit of the Loom, Jerzees.
- **Copyright date under the graphic** dates the *artwork*, not the shirt — the shirt
  is that year or a few years after. Never state the copyright year as the shirt's
  year; write "graphic copyright 1994, shirt is mid-90s".
- Combine stitching + tag + print technique + country + fit + fabric + copyright. If
  they all agree, be confident. If one disagrees, widen the range.

**Levi's:**
- **Big E vs small e**: tab reading `LEVI'S` all caps = **pre-1971**. `Levi's` with
  only the L capitalised = post-1971. The single most reliable tab marker.
- **Care tag**: single-language = 70s; multi-language with care codes = 80s–90s;
  detailed with lot numbers = late 90s. **No care tag** = pre-1971 or removed — be
  cautious and say so.
- **Lot code** inside the back pocket, upper left: style number (501/505/517) + fabric
  code + factory code. A **red** lot number on the back tag = **1986–1993**.
- **Top button reverse** carries a factory number (555, 501, 524, 6, 2, 16); consistent
  stamping depth and honest patina read as old.
- Four data points together narrow to a **3–5 year window** — that is the honest claim
  to make in a listing. Never claim a single year unless the tag states it.

## 4. How to write about authenticity without creating liability

**The principle:** a specific claim ("100% authentic") is an **express warranty**. An
"as-is" or "sold as photographed" line does **not** disclaim an express warranty you
made earlier in the same listing. So the safest listing is one that shows everything
and promises nothing.

**Never write** (hard ban, listings and messages both):
- "100% authentic", "guaranteed genuine", "authenticity guaranteed", "verified real"
- "Comes with COA" unless a real, named third-party report exists and is photographed
- "Legit checked" without naming who checked it and showing the report
- Any statement about what a future authenticator will conclude

**Write instead:**
- "Sourced from [where]. Stamps and hardware photographed unedited in photos 5–8 so you
  can check them against your own references."
- "Authenticated by [named service], report [number], photo 9."
- "I can send additional macro photos of any detail before you buy — ask and I'll shoot
  them the same day."
- "Sold as photographed and measured."

**If Alex cannot establish provenance:** the piece is not listed. Not at a discount,
not "as unbranded", not "inspired by". Listing a designer item with the brand name
removed to dodge review is still a counterfeit listing if the item is counterfeit, and
listing a genuine item as generic just loses money.

## 5. Shipping practice that survives a dispute

Depop provides **no shipping labels for Greece**. Alex arranges his own courier and
quotes a flat price. That means every protection has to be built by hand.

**Non-negotiable on every parcel:**
1. **Tracked.** Untracked from Greece is an open invitation; "item not received" is
   automatically lost without a scan. Untracked orders also pay out far later
   (reported: 10 working days after sale vs 2 business days after delivery for
   tracked, US figures).
2. **Ship only to the address on the order.** Never to a "friend's house", never to a
   changed address in a message, never to a different country. Shipping elsewhere
   voids protection entirely and is the most common single scam.
3. **Photo of the packed parcel with the label legible**, plus the carrier receipt.
4. **Post within 24 hours of sale** and send the tracking number in the Depop thread
   immediately. Late dispatch is the second-biggest claim generator after undisclosed
   flaws.
5. **Signature on delivery for anything over €300.** Required historically by PayPal
   above $750 and recommended since 26 Jan 2026; take it anyway.
6. **Insure to full value** on anything over €300.
7. **Declare the true value on the customs form.** Under-declaring to help a buyer
   dodge duty is customs fraud, it voids the insurance, and the app must refuse to
   suggest it even when the buyer asks. Say so once, politely, in writing.
8. **State duty responsibility in the listing**: "ships from Greece — customs duty or
   import VAT in your country is the buyer's responsibility." This prevents the single
   most common surprise-based negative review on cross-border sales.
9. **Pack so the parcel cannot be opened and resealed invisibly** — tape over seams,
   sign across the tape. This is what defeats an "empty box" claim.
10. **Never refund outside the app.** If the platform does not see the refund, the
    buyer can still open a dispute and be paid twice.

**Transit realism from Greece** (state as estimates, never promises):
Greece → UK roughly 5–8 working days; Greece → US roughly 8–14, plus customs.
Add 5–7 days to every holiday cut-off you read on a US or UK blog.

## 6. Red-flag buyer behaviours — escalate to Alex, do not transact

Any of these, the machine flags the thread and drafts a polite decline:
- Asks to pay off-platform, by friends-and-family, bank transfer, crypto, or gift card
- Asks to ship to a different address than the order
- Asks for the customs value to be under-declared or the parcel marked "gift"
- Pushes for a certificate of authenticity that does not exist
- Pressures for immediate dispatch at an unusual hour, or for dispatch before payment
  clears
- Brand-new account, no reviews, first purchase is the most expensive item in the shop
- Opens with a threat about reviews or disputes
- Has previously been refunded
- Asks Alex to "just mark it as sold" and settle elsewhere

**The switch scam** is the specific one to fear on Chrome Hearts and bags: buyer
receives the genuine item, returns a counterfeit, claims INAD. Defences: the unedited
turning video, macro hallmark photos taken before packing, a **tamper-evident seal or
security tag through a buttonhole photographed before shipping**, and the serial
number recorded. If a return arrives, **film the unboxing before cutting the tape**,
every time, no exceptions.
