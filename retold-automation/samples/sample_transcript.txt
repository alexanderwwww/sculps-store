Speaker A: Okay Nonna, it's recording now. Can you start by telling me where you were born?

Speaker B: Ah, you are recording me? Look at this. Okay, okay. I was born in nineteen forty-one, in a little village in Calabria, in the south of Italy. Very small place, up in the hills. My father, he worked the land, and my mother, she took care of us. I was the third of six children. Three girls, three boys.

Speaker A: Six kids. What was the house like?

Speaker B: The house was stone. Cold in the winter, you cannot imagine how cold, and cool in the summer, which was nice. We had one big room downstairs where we cooked and we ate and we lived, and upstairs we all slept together. It smelled always of two things — the bread my mother was baking, and the lemons. She kept the lemons in a bowl by the window. Even now, when I smell a lemon, I am back in that kitchen.

Speaker A: Were you poor?

Speaker B: We had nothing. Nothing! But I tell you the truth, Mia, I never once felt poor. My mother, she could make a feast out of nothing. A little flour, a little oil, some tomatoes that we dried on the roof in the sun. And everybody shared. That was the thing about the village. Nobody had anything, so everybody shared everything.

Speaker A: You were a little girl during the war. Do you remember it?

Speaker B: I remember the faces of the grown-ups more than I remember the war itself. The fear in their faces. When the soldiers came through the village, my mother, she put all of us children down in the cellar, with the wine barrels, and she told us to be quiet as mice. I did not understand what was happening. But I understood that my mother was afraid, and that frightened me more than anything.

Speaker A: That must have been so hard.

Speaker B: It was hard, but you know what it taught me? It taught me that people are stronger together than alone. During the war, the whole village became one family. If a child was hungry, any door would open. No door was ever locked to a hungry child. I carried that with me my whole life. That is why my kitchen was always open, you know this.

Speaker A: I do know that. Okay, so tell me how you came to America.

Speaker B: In nineteen sixty-one, I got on a ship. I was twenty years old. Twenty! A baby, really. I had one suitcase, one, and my mother had sewn the address of my cousin into the lining of my coat, in case I lost the paper. I was so seasick, eleven days on that ocean, I thought I would die. And every single night I cried for my mother.

Speaker A: Eleven days. Were you scared?

Speaker B: Terrified. But I remember, when we came into the harbor in New York, and we saw the statue, the Lady with the torch — everybody came up onto the deck. And there was an old man next to me, and he took off his hat, like this, and he held it to his chest. And I looked around at all these faces, and I understood something. We were all frightened, and we were all hopeful, at exactly the same time. All of us together. I never forgot that man and his hat.

Speaker A: And that's where you met Nonno?

Speaker B: Ha! Yes. I met your grandfather, Antonio, at a dance. In the church hall, on a Saturday. And I have to tell you the truth about him — he could not dance. Not at all! He stepped on my feet the entire night, poor man. But he made me laugh. Oh, he made me laugh. And at the end of the night he walked me home, and he took the long way, all the way around, so it would last longer. I knew then. I knew.

Speaker A: That's so romantic.

Speaker B: We were married within the year. We had nothing, again nothing — a rented room and a bed we bought second-hand from a man down the street. But I would not trade those first years for all the money in the world. We were so happy. We had so little and we were so happy.

Speaker A: And then you had the kids.

Speaker B: Four children in six years! Can you imagine? Your mother, and your uncles. I sewed all their clothes myself, on a little machine. And Antonio worked two jobs, sometimes three. But on Sunday — no matter what — on Sunday we all sat down together at one table and we ate. That was the rule. There was always music in that house, somebody was always singing, and there was always room for one more plate if a neighbor came by.

Speaker A: Tell me about the shop.

Speaker B: Ah, the shop. In nineteen seventy-one, we opened a little grocery store, on the corner. And I stood behind that counter for thirty years. Thirty years! I knew every customer by name, and I knew all their troubles too. And when somebody was struggling, I gave them credit. I wrote it in a book. Because I remembered, you see. I remembered being the one who had nothing. How could I say no?

Speaker A: Did people pay you back?

Speaker B: Most of them, eventually. Some never. It doesn't matter. That little shop, it put all four children through school. I am prouder of that shop than of almost anything in my whole life. Almost. The only thing I am prouder of is all of you.

Speaker A: Nonna. Can you tell me about when Nonno died?

Speaker B: [pause] Your grandfather passed in the spring of nineteen ninety-eight. We had forty years together, and I am telling you, it was not enough. It is never enough. For a long time after, the house was too quiet. I did not know what to do with all the quiet.

Speaker A: How did you get through it?

Speaker B: I learned something, in that time. I learned that grief is just love that has nowhere to go anymore. That's all it is. So I took all that love, and I gave it to you children, and to the grandchildren, and to my garden. I planted tomatoes, the way Antonio liked them. And slowly, slowly, the quiet became peaceful instead of empty. That took years. But it came.

Speaker A: That's beautiful, Nonna. Is there anything else you want people to know? Anything you want us to remember?

Speaker B: I am an old woman now. I have lived a very big life out of very small things. If you remember anything of me, remember this — be kind. Feed people. And never, never lock the door on somebody who is hungry, whether they are hungry for food or hungry for a little bit of love.

Speaker A: I will. I promise.

Speaker B: And one more thing. You children, all of you — you are the best thing I ever made. Better than the bread, better than the shop, better than anything. Do you hear me? So whenever you miss me, when I am gone, you come and you find my voice again, and I will still be here, telling you all of this. I am not going anywhere.

Speaker A: Okay. I love you, Nonna. That's a good place to stop.

Speaker B: I love you too, tesoro. Now turn that thing off and come eat something, you are too skinny.
