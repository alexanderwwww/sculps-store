# The eight panels

**Every product carries exactly eight thumbnails, and these are the eight.** In this
order. Nothing else goes in the gallery.

They are **panels, not photographs**. A panel is allowed to be full of text. It answers
its one question on its own face, so nobody has to read a description to understand it.

In all eight: square 1:1, the product **cut out and huge**, sharp and lit, sitting in
FRONT of the scene, the biggest thing in the frame. Never a small object lost in a wide
shot.

---

## 1. The hero — *what is this*

The product cut out and huge in the lower half. Behind it, dark, the real scene it lives
in — the house, the lawn, the room.

Across the **top**, a row of **five small bordered chips**, each with a thin white line
icon and two short lines of caps: the fact on top, the qualifier under it.

    3 FT LONG · 0.9 M
    BATTERIES · REMOTE
    RED LIT EYES · IT SCREAMS
    IT MOVES · CRAWLS FORWARD
    OUTDOOR · ANY LAWN

One headline under the chips, heavy condensed white caps, two to four words.

The chip row is what makes the image read as a real listing rather than an art photo. It
is the single most important panel and it is thumbnail number one.

## 2. What it does — *why do I want it*

The product cut out large on **one side**. Down the **other side**, five rows. Each row:
a thin white line icon in an accent-coloured circle, a short bold caps label, then one
plain sentence.

    ONE PLUG — Nothing to build. Point it and turn it on.
    TWELVE SCENES — Ghosts, witches, screaming faces, ready on it.

Headline in the top corner: EVERYTHING IT DOES, or the product's own promise.

## 3. How big — *will it work at my house*

Mostly empty, clean, evenly lit. The product with a **thin white measuring line with small
end ticks** along its full length or height, the figure in small caps beside it, and a
**flat grey silhouette of a standing adult at the same scale** next to it.

For anything that projects, throws or covers: draw the coverage as an accent-coloured
shape landing on an outlined rectangle, with its own measuring line.

Drawn the way an engineer draws it. No drama, no night, no atmosphere — this one is for
the part of the brain that is measuring a lawn.

## 4. How it runs — *what does it need from me*

The panel that kills the power question before it is asked. Close, daylight or bright
indoor light, the hands doing the thing: the battery cover sliding shut and the remote on
the grass; or the single plug going into the single socket.

Three small numbered steps across the bottom, each one picture-plus-one-line, or one
short caps line if the picture says it alone: **BATTERIES IN. NO CABLE.**

Whatever is true of the product is true in this panel. A battery product never shows a
cable, a plug or a socket. Ever.

## 5. How it works — *is this going to be a job*

Three numbered steps across the frame, each a small clear picture with one short line:

    1 STAKE IT DOWN   2 PLUG IT IN   3 WALK AWAY

Numbers in the accent colour, everything else white. This is the panel that makes a
nervous buyer feel capable.

## 6. What arrives — *am I going to be missing something*

Shot from **directly above** on a clean dark surface, evenly lit. Every item laid out
neatly and **separately**, with space between them. Each one has a thin white leader line
to its name in small caps.

    PROJECTOR · REMOTE · POWER LEAD · STAND · 12 SCENES

Headline across the top: EVERYTHING IN THE BOX.

The panel most often skipped, and the one that removes the last objection.

## 7. The detail — *is it any good*

No text, or one word. Very close on the part that proves the quality: the face, the
stitching, the lens, the lit eyes, the material. Lit so the texture reads.

A shopper who has understood the specification now wants to be reassured it is not
rubbish. This is that reassurance, and it is the panel that looks best at thumbnail size.

## 8. The feeling — *the dopamine*

**Almost no text.** One short line, or none.

A real scene with real people: the neighbours stopping, the kids backing away, the family
on the blanket, the street slowing down. Deep blacks, real depth, warm light. The product
is still the hero, but here the shopper is imagining themselves in the picture rather
than reading it.

The first seven sell the specification. This one sells the reason.

---

## Thumbnails

The thumbnail is the panel at 64px. Before accepting an image, shrink it and look:

- Is the product still recognisable as a shape?
- Is the headline still readable, or has it turned into grey texture?
- Would this stop a thumb on a phone?

If the answer is no, the product is too small or the type is too fine. The chip row
usually survives; a long sentence never does.

## Bundle row pictures

The bundle boxes stay exactly as they are. Their **little picture** gets the same
treatment: square, the product cut out and filling the square, edge to edge, no inset.
One figure against two figures, so the difference between ONE and TWO reads without the
label being read.
