# organicx

An **organic dropshipping operator** that runs as an app on Alex's dock, the same
way Magic Wand does. It is an agent with hands: it drives a real browser and a
real phone, with permission, and it does the job a person would do.

**It is not a Halloween app.** Nothing about a store, a niche or a product is
hardcoded. You point it at a product — a link, or a product already in Shop
Admin — and everything downstream follows from that. Right now it is pointed at
Black Reaper for one month. Next month it is pointed somewhere else and nothing
about it changes.

That is the first lesson from Magic Wand: version one had one shop baked into
it, and every fix after that was undoing the assumption.

## Platforms

**TikTok, Instagram and YouTube. All of them, all vertical, all reels.** One
product, one account per platform, the same clip cut for each.

Ranking for effort when something has to give, as of 2026:

| Platform | Weight | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Instagram Reels** | highest | Best conversion, product tagging in the reel, one tap to the store, and the biggest buying intent |
| **Facebook Reels** | high | Same tagging, same buying power, older audience that TikTok never reaches |
| **YouTube Shorts** | medium | Weaker conversion, but you get paid for views even when a product does not convert, and product tagging works and nobody uses it |
| **TikTok** | lowest | TikTok Shop has pushed organic sellers down. Still worth posting; no longer worth leading with |

A product whose buyer is a 55-year-old woman does not exist on TikTok. Running
TikTok only throws away most of the catalogue.

## Why it exists right now

Meta paid ads are the main channel. **Organic is not competing with that** — it
is doing two things paid cannot:

1. **Warming brand-new accounts.** The Instagram and TikTok profiles have no
   followers and no history. Every post teaches those platforms who this account
   is for, so that when the algorithm starts recommending it, it recommends it
   to the right people. That takes weeks, and there is over a month to do it.
2. **Free sales along the way.** Anything that converts while the account warms
   is margin with no ad spend against it.

Reposting other creators' clips is how a new account gets off zero. That is the
accepted trade, and the inspector's provenance check is what keeps it from
costing an account rather than building one.

## The loop

1. **Understand the product** — from a link or from the store. What it is, what
   it looks like, what it does, what people call it.
2. **Research** — find creators already posting this product or products like it,
   across all three platforms.
3. **Judge** — not "here is a video" but *this has 400k views, 2k comments, and
   people are asking where to buy*. Validation signals, read the way a buyer
   reads them.
4. **Pull it clean** — download without the watermark.
5. **Write the caption and the hook.**
6. **The inspector approves or rejects** — a second agent doing Alex's job.
7. **Post**, on a human schedule.

All of that sits inside a day of ordinary behaviour — watching, liking,
following, commenting — driven by the account's persona. Posting is one thing
the account does, not the only thing. See `references/behaviour.md`.

### The inspector

Nothing goes out without it. It checks four things:

- **Is this actually our product?** Not a lookalike, not last year's version.
- **Is the creative any good?** Hook in the first second, clear what is being
  sold, a call to action that exists.
- **Is there real evidence this format works?** Views, comments, saves — proof
  of concept, not a guess.
- **Provenance.** Is this supplier footage, is it reusable, has this exact clip
  already been posted into the ground, is the watermark crop obvious.

That last one is not caution for its own sake. **A copyright strike does not
shadowban an account, it kills it** — and duplicate detection is very good at
spotting a reposted clip with the corner cut off. Strike risk *is* ban risk, and
the inspector is already standing there.

## The two methods

**Copy method** — take clips that already exist, cut them, run many products.
Zero inventory, zero wait, five products tested in the time the other method
tests one. This is the default.

**Classic organic** — order the product, film it yourself. Slower (two to three
weeks before the box even lands in Greece) but the algorithm genuinely prefers
footage shot on a phone, and no competitor can copy what you shot.

The ideal is a mix: run copy, and the moment a product shows proof of concept,
order it and start filming.

## What matters, in order

1. **Mindset** — speed and focus. Everything else is downstream.
2. **Product research** — 90% of the time. This is the whole competitive edge.
3. **Creatives** — iterations on something already proven.
4. **Timing and consistency.**
5. **Customer support** (once it scales).
6. **The store** — barely matters. See `references/store.md`.

## The three rules that decide everything

**Speed.** See, decide, move. Not ten days choosing the perfect product — that
is fear dressed as research. The moment a product has even two validations, run
it. Nobody knows it will sell, including the person who found it. Only the test
knows.

**The five-day rule.** Every product gets five to seven days. No traction, no
views, no movement — cut it, no emotion, next product. The thing that kills
people is not testing the wrong product; it is staying on one product for a
month because their ego went into it.

**The math.** ~5% of tested products win, so roughly **20 tests per winner**.
Twenty is fixed. The only variable is how fast you get to twenty. Test 20 a
month and you find a winner every month; test 6 a month and you wait three. Same
skill, same luck — only speed differs.

Full reasoning in `references/mindset.md`.

## How it is driven

**The playbook ships inside the app, not only inside Claude.** Every reference
in this skill is compiled into OrganicX itself, so the crew can work without
asking permission for each decision. Claude holds the same copy, which is what
keeps the two in step.

So the command is big, not small:

> *"Black Reaper, the Crawling Zombie. Run it for five days across all three
> platforms. Warm the accounts as you go."*

and then the agency gets on with it — on **Alex's machine, using his compute**.
Reyna researches, Desmond kills most of it, Lena cuts, the gate signs or
refuses, Sam posts, Theo reads the numbers back. Nobody stops to ask what a
validation is, because they already know.

Claude is not in the loop for every step. Claude is in the loop for:

- setting the brief and the boundaries
- anything the gate refuses that Alex should see
- anything that needs a decision only he can make
- fixing the app when something is wrong, and shipping that fix to it

That split is the point. Round-tripping every like and every caption would be
slower than doing it by hand, which would defeat the entire object.

## App architecture

Same shape as Magic Wand, with one addition.

- **Runs on the Mac and the iPhone.** Accounts can be added from either. Start a
  run from the phone, set a timer, leave it going.
- **Has hands.** Drives a real browser and the phone's own apps, with permission,
  visibly, and stoppable at any moment.
- **Queue → inspector → approve → publish.** Alex sees everything before it goes
  out, the same way Magic Wand asks before it draws.
- **Reports what it is doing**, in plain language, the way the wand's status does.
- **Updates itself.** Version one ships with the update channel in it. Magic Wand
  reached build 71 because every fix meant a rebuild and a reinstall; this one
  takes fixes without Alex doing anything.
- **Carries a persona per account**, written once and kept, so no two accounts
  behave alike.

### Learned from Magic Wand — do not repeat

- **A job must carry an id.** A job without one was silently refused while the
  app reported the same message it gives for an empty queue. An hour was lost to
  that once.
- **A queued job is not a running job.** After queueing, confirm the status shows
  *your* job name — it will happily resume the previous one instead.
- **Never generate imagery that purports to show the physical product.** Alex
  rejected AI flat-lays of box contents outright, and he was right: they showed
  parts the product does not ship. Generated imagery is for **scenes** — a yard,
  a lit window, a house at night — always with the real product as reference.
  Anything claiming to show the product itself must be real.
- **Say what failed and why.** "Nothing rendered" is not a status.

## House rules that apply here too

- Money off is always **dollars**, never percentages.
- **Never a review count.**
- No fake urgency, no invented scarcity, no fabricated reference price.
- Never dummy or sample data.
- Never post the same asset twice to the same account.
- Never claim a result that has not been measured.

## Things already settled — do not re-derive them

**The dock icon is sized by looking, not by the grid.** Apple's 824-of-1024
figure is drawn for a hard-edged rounded square. The OrganicX icon is a
squircle whose glass edges taper, so the same bounding box carries less visual
mass and reads visibly smaller in the dock next to Magic Wand. It sits at
**1000 of 1024**. Measuring the alpha bbox said the two were the same size;
the eye said otherwise, and the eye was right. When an icon looks wrong,
render it beside the wand at 128px and look — do not measure and argue.

**A double-clicked .app cannot see Homebrew.** It inherits launchd's PATH
(`/usr/bin:/bin:/usr/sbin:/sbin`) and nothing more, so `node`, `ffmpeg` and
`yt-dlp` are invisible even though they work in Terminal. The launcher puts
`/opt/homebrew/bin` and `/usr/local/bin` on PATH, asks `brew --prefix`, and
sources the login shell for version managers. Getting this wrong produces a
confident, wrong alert — "Node is not installed" on a Mac where it is.

**The panel and cursor are template literals.** A backtick or a `${` inside
them, *including inside a comment*, breaks them at a line nowhere near the
cause. `node --check` after every edit, and the test suites compile the
overlay separately for exactly this reason.

**Find elements by id, never by sniffing inline styles.** Style-sniffing has
produced a confident wrong answer twice: once finding the test page's own
button and declaring the stop broken, once reading a computed opacity
mid-transition and declaring the label broken. Every overlay element carries
an id.

**Research never runs in a logged-in context.** It is a structural guard with
a test, not a convention. The failure it prevents is silent — research from an
account's browser works perfectly and costs that account three weeks later.
