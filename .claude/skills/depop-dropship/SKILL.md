# Depop dropshipping — the method

Distilled from four sellers' own walkthroughs, September 2026. Numbers are
theirs; where they disagree it says so.

Alex's edge over every one of them: **he can source anything, authentically, in
real life.** They are all reselling the same AliExpress catalogue as each other.
He is not. That changes which parts of this apply to him and which are beneath
him.

## Why the model works at all

Depop has **56 million users** and is a secondhand platform, so almost nobody
pays for ads — which means Depop distributes listings organically, for free.
There is no traffic cost. That is the whole leverage: a normal dropshipping
store pays for every visitor, this one does not.

The corollary, said by all four: the competition is **terrible**. Supplier
photos, shrivelled garments, two-line descriptions, sellers who do not even ship.
And they still sell. The bar is on the floor.

## 1. Pick the person before the product

The most repeated mistake: choosing what to sell before choosing who buys it.

> "It does not matter what you're selling. Pick someone you are selling to."

Pick a **person**, not a niche. Niches are usually invented and seasonal — a
beach niche dies in November. A person does not. Y2K girls, dark academia,
Miami girls, emo kids, history teachers. Then the profile picture, the bio, the
backgrounds, the mockups and the copy are all curated for that one person, so
they arrive and think *this is my shop*.

Then **one product type**. Not hoodies and jeans and bags and rings. One. A
store selling everything reads as a random online thrift shop and nobody buys
from those.

Brand account vs person account: a **brand** charges more and scales into
something real, but Depop is built for people selling to people, so it needs
real consistency. A **person** account is easier, more forgiving of variation,
harder to charge a premium on. Start as a person unless the brand is the point.

The profile picture should be **aimed at the demographic**, and it does not have
to be his face — most real sellers use something off Pinterest. A mismatched
picture kills trust before the listing is read.

## 2. Product research — demand signals, not vanity

The method all four use:

1. Search any term for the category.
2. **Set brand to "Other."** This filters to unbranded goods, which is where
   the dropshippers live.
3. Optionally set a **minimum price of ~£20**, which filters out genuine
   secondhand and leaves dropshipped items.
4. Open **every** result.
5. Judge only on: **"in N people's bags"** and **"offers sent."**

> "Likes is okay, but likes doesn't really prove that anyone bought it."

Bags and offers are money moving. Likes are not. Something with 26 likes and
zero bags is dead; 4 bags and 10 offers sent is proven demand.

6. **Reverse-image the winner into AliExpress** to find the source and the cost.
   That gives the margin before committing.

Worked example from the transcripts: bag sourced at **$17–19**, selling at
**$50**. Another: sourced **£14.27**, selling **£26** — double, every time.

## 3. Mockups — the actual bottleneck

Said more forcefully than anything else in all four videos.

**Never use the supplier's photos.** Not AliExpress's, not Alibaba's, not Temu's,
and *not the customer review pictures either* — every dropshipper knows that
trick, so duplicates of the same image appear across competing listings. Depop
keeps a catalogue of those images. Posting them is how accounts get flagged.

Instead: **generate the mockups** from the product photos, with AI, and make
them

- **realistic** — a scrolling buyer has to stop, believe it is a real item, and
  click. Without that, no sales, full stop.
- **accurate** to what actually arrives, or the refunds come.
- **consistent** — the same background, the same light, across the whole store.

> "When you go to the store and they're all consistent, it looks like a real
> human being put in effort. A bunch of random pictures on random models — even
> if you can't think about it consciously, your subconscious tells you this is
> sketchy."

Several angles per item. One flat-lay hero, then the same object from other
angles, on the same surface.

## 4. Warming the account

A new account has a trust score of zero. Start dropshipping on day one and it is
banned on day one — and Depop **IP-bans**, so the next account dies too.

The warm-up, roughly **one week**:

- browse, like, follow, behave like a person
- **list three or four things you actually own, sell them, collect the reviews**

An **old account with real history and reviews can skip all of this** and start
immediately — and is worth far more than a new one. One seller warmed nothing
because his account had sat dormant for years and it was fine.

## 5. Listing cadence — the sources disagree, and the gap matters

- Account **under 6 months old: no more than ~5 listings a week.** More and the
  ban risk is real.
- Account **over 6 months, with history: effectively unlimited.**
- A different seller says **5–10 a day** when starting, because listing is free
  and nothing teaches you what works except volume.

**Resolution: the age of the account decides, not the ambition.** Take the
conservative number while the account is young. The one who said 5–10 a day was
posting on an account he had and never used. Volume without age is how the bans
happen, and a banned account takes the money with it.

## 6. The listing itself

- **Condition: "like new" rather than "brand new."** Brand-new on a secondhand
  platform draws a second look from Depop's automated checks.
- **Size:** for menswear, medium and large sell most. It costs nothing to list
  the common sizes — the supplier ships whatever is ordered.
- **Description:** written fresh. Depop's automated checks flag descriptions
  that read as a bot or duplicate another listing. Say what the thing is, the
  size, the fit, the material, the condition.
- Fill the optional attributes (material, fit, style). They are search signals.
- **Boost** is Depop's paid push. Optional, and it takes a cut. Not the lever to
  reach for first.

## 7. Fulfilment

An order arrives → take the buyer's name and address from Depop → place the
order with the supplier → ship direct. AliExpress packaging is unbranded.

One seller waits **2–3 days before marking it shipped** so the buyer does not
notice the gap. *(See the refusals — this one is deception, not technique.)*

## 8. Niche down on what actually sold

The only real analysis in the whole method, and it is sound:

> "Two items that did great and they're both emo? Upload more emo. Two that did
> nothing and they're both hats? Upload fewer hats."

After the first weeks the store tells you what it is. Double down on the
category that converted, cut the rest. That is how a scattered page becomes a
shop with a point of view.

## 9. Clean up, then scale

Before adding volume, fix everything already broken, because scale multiplies
problems as fast as revenue: drop suppliers who send the wrong item, delete
items that generate disputes, fix slow replies. Then scale — and the sellers who
go past ~5K/month do it by **running several stores**, not by cramming one.

## 10. The honest ceiling

The most credible of the four says it plainly: this is a **low-margin model if
run casually**. Seven days of real numbers from a beginner:

- **$438 revenue, $163 costs, $264 net** in the first week
- another: **$525 revenue over 7 days, ~$51/day profit**

The ones claiming 8K/month are selling mentorships and supplier lists. Treat
those numbers as marketing until proven otherwise.

**Where Alex is different:** every seller above is marking a $17 bag up to $50.
He can source real goods across the entire price range. One authentic piece at
€400 with a €150 spread beats forty tote bags, and it is a shop with a reason to
exist rather than a mirror of the same AliExpress catalogue everyone else is
reselling.

# What flip will not do

Four things appear in these videos that are not aggressive tactics, they are the
end of the business. flip refuses them, and the refusal is in the code, not just
in this file.

### Faking the account's country

One seller sets his country of origin to the **United Kingdom** while living in
the US, specifically so Depop lets him use his own shipping label instead of
theirs, and so he gets paid without Depop's delivery confirmation.

That is deliberately defeating the platform's payment protection by lying about
where he is. It is fraud on Depop and on every buyer. When it is caught the
account goes and the balance goes with it, and the balance is the point.
**flip will not set or suggest a false country.**

### Replicas

"Balenciaga hoodie, $30 from the supplier, list at £500." That is a counterfeit,
sold as genuine. A third seller says quietly that reps "make a bunch of money
but you will get banned — delete them before you scale," which tells you they
know.

Counterfeit trafficking is a crime, not a policy violation. It ends in seized
funds, chargebacks, and for a real business a legal problem. **flip will not
list a replica, and when sourcing is ambiguous it asks rather than assumes.**

This matters most precisely because Alex *can* get the real thing. His whole
advantage disappears the moment the shop carries one fake.

### Copying a competitor's photos and description

> "Literally just copy the description of this one."

It is someone else's copyright, Depop detects duplicates, and it puts the shop
in the same image catalogue everyone else is in. The mockup section of this same
method says exactly why that is fatal. **flip writes its own, always.**

### Lying about the item's history

"Worn once." "Never worn." Written on an item that has never been in his hands,
to imply it is his own secondhand piece.

It is small, and it is the exact sentence a buyer quotes in a not-as-described
claim. **flip describes the item truthfully.** New from supplier is new from
supplier, and it says so.

# What this means for the app

Every section above is a job flip has to be able to do. In order:

1. **Research** — run the search, set brand to Other, open the results, read
   bags and offers per listing, rank by proven demand, reverse-image to a source
   and a cost, and put the shortlist on his board with the margin already
   worked out.
2. **Mockups** — take the product photos and produce a consistent set in the
   store's own look, several angles, same surface and light every time. This is
   the single highest-leverage thing the app can do, because it is the stated
   bottleneck and it is pure work.
3. **Listing** — write the title, the description, the attributes, the price
   against the source cost, and fill Depop's form up to the last screen. He
   presses List.
4. **Cadence** — hold the account-age rule as a hard cap, and tell him when he
   has hit it rather than letting him walk into a ban.
5. **Answer** — reply inside four hours, negotiate down to a floor he sets.
6. **Refresh and offer** — inside the windows, on the most-liked, never on dead
   stock.
7. **Learn** — track which listings sold, cluster them, and say what the shop
   actually is: "your emo pieces sell, your hats don't."
8. **Fulfil** — take the buyer's details, put the order in with the supplier,
   track it. He confirms.

The pressing stays his. Not because the app is timid, but because a shop that
behaves like a bot dies, and this one has to still be here in a year.
