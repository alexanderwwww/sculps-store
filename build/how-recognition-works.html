How the store knows who walked in

There are only three moments where you actually learn who someone is. Everything else is the system remembering what it was already told.

The customer sees this Happens by itself, invisible
1

Someone lands on your store. You know nothing about them.

A stranger. You have their rough city and their internet provider, and that is genuinely all. No name, no email, no phone.

Nothing happens
2

They type their email to buy.

This is the moment. Every order needs an email for the receipt, so it arrives accurate and it arrives free. This is where almost all of your customer list comes from.

You receive: email, phone, name, address
3

Catch the email before they reach the address step.

Put the email field first and save it the moment it is a valid address — before they finish, before they pay. Most people leave at the shipping form. This is how you keep the ones who almost bought.

Abandoned cart, recoverable
4

You leave a tag in their browser.

A long random string — not their email — set by that store, for that store. Their details stay in your database; the browser only carries the tag that points to them.

Invisible. Nothing to click.
5

They come back to the same store.

The tag is recognised and the shop greets them by name. Nothing travels between your stores: each one keeps its own customers, its own list, its own tag. A shopper on one brand has no way to tell it shares an owner with another.

6

Checkout is already filled in.

Name, email, phone, shipping address — all there, and they never logged in. To them it feels like the shop remembered them. To you, it is a customer you can email, because they gave you the address themselves.

The thing that does not work

An IP address cannot be turned into an email or a phone number. There is no system anywhere that holds that link and lets you query it. Companies that sell you "visitor emails" are matching guesses against lists bought from data brokers — around a third of them are the wrong person, it is illegal in Europe outright, and the spam complaints it generates are exactly what costs a store its payment processor.

Amazon is not doing this either. Amazon knows you because you are signed into an Amazon account.

So what do you actually build?

Visible — almost nothing

  • The email field moved to the top of checkout
  • A "welcome back" line for a returning shopper
  • A privacy policy line, and a way to be forgotten

Invisible — the plumbing

  • customers, carts and orders tables, each scoped to one store
  • A signed tag per store — expiring, revocable
  • Carts saved from the first valid email, so the abandoned ones are reachable

One rule that shapes all of it

Stores never share customers. Each brand stands on its own: its own list, its own tag, its own database rows. Nothing on the page and nothing in a shopper's browser reveals that two of your shops have the same owner — and no shared list exists to leak, be subpoenaed, or be inherited by a buyer if you ever sell one of them.
