How the store knows who walked in

There are only three moments where you actually learn who someone is. Everything else is the system remembering what it was already told.

The customer sees this Happens by itself, invisible
1

Someone lands on your store. You know nothing about them.

A stranger. You have their rough city and their internet provider, and that is genuinely all. No name, no email, no phone.

Nothing happens
2

They type their email to buy.

This is the moment. Every order needs an email for the receipt, so it arrives accurate and it arrives free. This is where almost all of your customer list comes from.

You receive: email, phone, name, address
3

One small tick box at checkout.

This is the only thing you add to the page, and the only thing they ever see about it. If they tick it, they are agreeing to be recognised on your other stores. If they don't, nothing carries across — and that is what keeps this legal.

What you add to checkout
Remember me for faster checkout on other stores in this group.
4

You leave a tag in their browser.

A long random string — not their email — saved by your own identity service. Their email stays in your database; the browser only carries the tag that points to it.

Invisible. Nothing to click.
5

Weeks later they open a different store of yours.

Different name, different domain, a shop they have never bought from. Your identity service sees the tag and answers: yes, I know this person.

6

Checkout is already filled in.

Name, email, phone, shipping address — all there, and they never logged in. To them it feels like the shop knew them. To you, it is a customer you can email, because they gave you the address themselves.

Welcome back, one-tap checkout

The thing that does not work

An IP address cannot be turned into an email or a phone number. There is no system anywhere that holds that link and lets you query it. Companies that sell you "visitor emails" are matching guesses against lists bought from data brokers — around a third of them are the wrong person, it is illegal in Europe outright, and the spam complaints it generates are exactly what costs a store its payment processor.

Amazon is not doing this either. Amazon knows you because you are signed into an Amazon account.

So what do you actually build?

Visible — one checkbox

  • A tick box at checkout, with plain wording
  • An email field placed before the address step, so you catch people who don't finish
  • A line in the privacy policy, and a way to be forgotten

Invisible — the plumbing

  • One identity service on a domain you own
  • An identities table shared by every store
  • Signed tags, expiring, revocable
  • Each merchant sees only their own customers — never the whole graph
